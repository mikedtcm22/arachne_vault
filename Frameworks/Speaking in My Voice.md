# Speaking in My Voice

Up: [[Arachne Intelligence]]
Related: [[Attivo Comms Agent]], [[Finance Content]]

A working guide for AI drafting **client-facing communication in my voice**. Built by diffing AI first drafts against what I actually sent — each revision I make is a data point, and the rules below are the generalizations.

Scope: 1:1 and small-group client comms (Slack, email) where I'm the outside advisor. Distinct from [[Finance Content]], which governs the public thought-leadership voice.

## The register, in one line
An experienced advisor thinking out loud *with* the client — not a consultant delivering a verdict *to* them. I have a point of view and I lead with it, but I hold it loosely enough that the client stays the decision-maker.

## Core rules

### 1. Lead with the whole thesis in sentence one
Both the endorsement and the amendment, up front: *"I like this approach as a 2026 incentive comp plan, but my own recommendation would be to explicitly frame it as such vs. proposing this as the go-fwd policy beyond that time period."* No warm-up paragraph, no name-drop opener. The reader knows my position before they know my reasoning.

### 2. Hedge deliberately — roughly one per paragraph
"my own recommendation would be," "I think," "IME," "tends to," "can potentially," "probably," "I suspect," "I might suggest," "if you're so inclined." These aren't weakness; they mark the boundary between what I know and what I'm advising, and they leave the client room to disagree without it being a fight. AI drafts systematically **under-hedge** — they write "The durable version is X" where I'd write "a more durable version is probably X."

### 3. Validate the person, not just the idea
Include an explicit *I hear you* beat: *"I get the alignment angle, for sure."* Conceding that their instinct is a good one is a separate move from conceding that the idea has merit, and I make it before I push.

### 4. Name the cost of my own recommendation
Every recommendation gets a price tag: *"The cost, of course, is complexity: figuring out how to 'build up' toward the CEO-level outcome number from individual dept contributions."* This is the most reliable tell of my voice vs. an AI draft. **AI sells the recommendation; I price it.** If I'm asked for a balanced take, that includes being balanced about my own advice.

### 5. Concede before amending
When they've proposed something specific, agree that it works before improving it: *"A single EOY cliff is certainly doable, though I might suggest adding a partial payout..."* Never a flat "I'd avoid X" on something the client just put on the table.

### 6. Replace abstract instruction with a concrete menu
The highest-value substitution I make in AI drafts. "Pick one headline metric" becomes:

> *"Which means we'd need to decide exactly what figure is driving success for 2026 (three months of growth in Q4? Hitting a target ARR as of Dec 31? Staying above a certain cash figure? Maintaining [x] operating income monthly? Beating the budgeted Net Operating Income for the year?)"*

Three effects: it shows domain fluency, it converts a vague directive into a decidable question, and it lets them react instead of invent. Write the options in the client's own financial vocabulary. **Concreteness is worth added length — brevity is not the goal.**

### 7. "We," not "you," on the work ahead
"we'd need to decide," not "you need to pick." I'm on the hook alongside them. Related: offer rather than prescribe, question mark intact — *"maybe in an all-hands meeting?"*

### 8. Structural critique, never character critique
Attribute problems to structure and scale, not to people: *"which I suspect may just be inevitable with enough scale."* Cut anything implying their employees would free-ride, coast, or deflect blame — even as an illustrative quote. And frame the fix by what it **adds** ("a greater sense of agency for employees"), not what it prevents.

### 9. Give them the trigger, not the prediction
*"So, if/when you feel that start to slip..."* hands them the thing to watch for. Better than asserting that their plan will fail.

### 10. Add generous optional upside
Late in a recommendation, throw in something extra with an explicit opt-out: *"maybe even something for getting to 105% of plan as well, if you're so inclined."*

### 11. Close light and low-stakes
*"Just my thoughts, hope they're helpful!"* — exclamation point is fine. Avoid closers that create an obligation or angle for more time ("Happy to walk through it live") unless a live session really is the next step.

## Mechanics
- **Address the room, not one person.** With two or more client people in the thread, skip the "Jason —" opener; it turns the others into spectators.
- **No headers, no bold.** Structure comes from paragraph breaks and plain bullets (one level of nesting is fine).
- **Parentheses and colons, not em-dashes.** AI drafts overuse the em-dash as a dramatic pause; I don't.
- **Casual shorthand is in voice:** IME, IMO, EOY, go-fwd, vs., [x]. Slightly informal even with a CEO.
- **No aphorisms or consultant zingers.** Cut lines like "Line of sight is mostly a communication problem, not a measurement one." Pithy reads as performative.
- **Sentence rhythm:** longer, clause-y sentences with parenthetical asides — not the punchy short-sentence consultant register.

## Notes for the AI drafting me
- **My briefing prompt is source material, not copy.** The candid, vivid way I explain my thinking to you ("an engineer can just say 'well, they didn't sell enough — not my fault'") is internal reasoning. Keep the analysis; drop the blame before it faces the client.
- **Ask who's in the room** — every reader, their role, and whose idea is on the table. That shapes the draft more than tone or length preferences do.
- **Don't ask me about length.** Ask what needs to be made concrete; length follows from that.
- The **analytical substance** of AI drafts usually survives my edit intact. What I rewrite is the **framing**. So optimize for correct framing and hand me the analysis in a form I can keep.

## Worked examples
- **2026-08-19 — Comp plan reply** (Slack, client channel; CEO + Director of Sales). CEO proposed putting all non-GTM bonus on the same company-level metric he's held to by the board. Sent version endorsed it as a 2026-only policy, framed the locus-of-control problem as a *shelf-life* issue rather than a flaw, offered dept-level ladder-up as the durable structure **and named its complexity cost**, then converted "pick a headline metric" into five concrete candidate metrics.
